What $399 of Press Release Distribution Actually Buys a Dental Practice

A step by step guide to writing, submitting and using a press release through PressRanger, built on the real placement report from a live distribution. Including the top 25 placements, the honest truth about the links, and why the spend is still worth making.

Start with the receipt

Most articles about press release distribution are written by people selling press release distribution. This one starts with an actual placement report from an actual dental practice release, and every number in it comes from that report.

Nine hundred and fifty placements for three hundred and ninety-nine dollars. Forty-two cents each.

That is a real number and it is the reason this is worth writing about. It is also the number that gets used to sell something that is not quite what it appears to be, so the next section deals with that before anything else.

The honest version, before we go any further

Press release distribution is sold to dental practices as link building. That pitch is wrong, and if you buy on that basis you will be disappointed and you will blame the wrong thing.

What Google actually says

This is not a matter of interpretation. Google names press releases specifically in its spam policy.

SourceGoogle Search Central, Spam policies for Google web search · developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies

Read the two quotes together and the position is clear. A paid press release link that passes ranking credit violates the policy. A nofollowed one does not violate the policy and also passes no ranking credit.

PressRanger's own documentation states that most of their links are nofollow. That is them complying with the rules, which is the correct thing for them to do. It also means the link building pitch was never the real value.

John Mueller of Google put it more bluntly in a 2018 discussion: Google tries to ignore links from press releases, because companies put the press releases out themselves.

SourceSearch Engine Journal, Are press releases good for SEO · www.searchenginejournal.com/are-press-releases-good-for-seo/38…
And the other side of the honesty

There is also no credible study showing that press release distribution does nothing. There is simply no rigorous study either way.

So be suspicious of anyone who tells you it lifts rankings, and equally suspicious of anyone who tells you it is worthless. Both are making claims the evidence does not support. What follows is the case built only on what can be verified.

So what is the $399 actually buying?

Five things. All of them real, none of them link equity.

The first two are the ones that matter most and they are worth expanding, because they are the least understood.

Entity signal, explained without jargon

Search engines and AI systems do not just index pages. They build a model of things in the world, and one of those things is your practice. That model is assembled from repeated mentions across many sources.

When nine hundred and fifty pages appear on the same day, all containing your practice name, your city, your services and your phone number, in consistent wording, that is a substantial amount of new evidence about who you are and where you are. It is not a vote for your rankings in the way a link is. It is corroboration of your existence and your context.

For a local business this matters more than for a national one, because Google names prominence as one of its three local ranking factors, and describes it as being based partly on how well known a business is across the web.

SourceGoogle Business Profile Help, Improve your local ranking on Google · support.google.com/business/answer/7091

Credibility, which is not a soft benefit for a dentist

A patient considering a full arch implant case is making a five figure decision about their own body. They will search your name. What they find shapes what they decide.

A practice whose name appears on Yahoo Finance, Business Insider, and a dozen broadcast network affiliate sites reads differently from one whose entire online presence is its own website and a Yelp page. That is not a ranking factor. It is the thing that happens after the ranking, which is the part that actually produces revenue.

A note on the AI claims

Every press distribution service now markets AI visibility. PressRanger's version is called AIWire and it is included in the Gold tier. The claim is that distributed content becomes part of the knowledge base that AI chatbots draw on.

The mechanism is plausible. The scale is smaller than the marketing suggests, and it is worth seeing the real numbers before you make it your reason to buy.

SourcePew Research Center, Google users are less likely to click on links when an AI summary appears · www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-le…
SourceTow Center for Digital Journalism, Columbia, We compared eight AI search engines · www.cjr.org/tow_center/we-compared-eight-ai-search-engines-the…

Pew analysed nearly sixty-nine thousand searches and found news websites made up five percent of sources cited in AI Overviews. Exactly the same five percent they represent in normal search results. News is not an outsized AI citation source.

The Tow Center ran sixteen hundred queries across eight AI search tools and found they answered more than sixty percent incorrectly, and that they frequently cited a syndicated copy of a story rather than the original.

Treat AI visibility as a bonus that may compound over the next few years, not as a reason to spend today. The entity and credibility arguments stand on their own.

The part that actually determines whether this works

Everything so far has been about distribution. Distribution is the cheap part and the easy part. The angle is what decides whether nine hundred and fifty placements do anything at all.

The newsworthiness test

The test in one sentence: could a local reporter write a story from this without adding anything?

If the answer is no, the release will still be distributed and still be indexed. It just will not be read by a human being, which removes four of the five benefits and leaves you with entity signal alone. Entity signal alone is worth something, but it is not worth what you could get.

Twelve angles a dental practice can genuinely use

The fifth category on that list, data you own, is the one almost nobody uses and the one that most reliably produces real coverage.

A practice that can look across its own patient records and say something true and specific has a story no competitor can copy and no wire service can manufacture. How many emergency appointments happen in the week after a public holiday. What proportion of patients in the area delayed treatment for more than a year. How many cracked teeth the practice sees during a particular sport's season.

Report it anonymously and in aggregate, obviously. But a local reporter with a column to fill will take a local data story over a practice announcement every single time.

Writing it

A press release has a fixed structure. It is not a creative format and deviating from it is the fastest way to get rejected or ignored.

SourcePurdue Online Writing Lab, Writing press releases · owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_jou…

Paragraph by paragraph

The inverted pyramid, and why it exists

Everything in a release is ordered by importance, descending. Most important facts in the first paragraph, context next, background last.

The reason is practical and it is about a hundred and fifty years old. An editor with a column of space cuts from the bottom. If your most important fact is in the last paragraph, it gets cut. If your release is structured so that any bottom portion can be removed and the story still works, it survives editing.

SourcePurdue Online Writing Lab, The inverted pyramid · owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_jou…

Formatting conventions that signal you know what you are doing

  • Dateline in the format CITY, State abbreviation, followed by a dash, then straight into the lead. For example: SPRINGFIELD, Ill. followed by the first sentence.
  • "For Immediate Release" at the top, or a specific embargo date if you have a reason for one. If you use an embargo, make both the date and the required action unambiguous.
  • Under one page. Purdue's guidance is explicit that anything past two pages starts reading as a news article rather than a release, which is a category error.
  • Media contact block with a name, a title, a phone number and an email. Not a general enquiries address.
  • Identical boilerplate every single time. This is the detail that turns a series of releases into a consistent entity signal rather than a series of unrelated documents.

What gets a release rejected

The healthcare constraint, which is specific to you

Wire services apply additional scrutiny to health content, and they are right to. Anything that reads as a clinical outcome claim will be flagged or rejected.

Say what you did, not what it achieves. "The practice now offers a same day crown service" is fine. "Patients experience less pain and better outcomes" is a claim you would need evidence for, and it will cost you the distribution.

PressRanger's own documentation notes that individual outlets maintain their own exclusion lists, and gives Yahoo Finance rejecting law office releases as an example. Ask before you assume.

Submitting it

This is the mechanical part and it is the easy part. Budget half an hour.

Registering, step by step

  1. Go to pressranger.com and create a free account. There is no cost and no card required. Wholesale distribution pricing is only visible once you are signed in, which is why the public pages show retail figures of $699 and $1,099.
  2. Verify the email and complete the company profile. Practice name, website, contact details. This becomes the sender identity on your releases so get the spelling right.
  3. Open the distribution section and choose your tier. The comparison table earlier in this guide covers what separates them.
  4. Paste or upload the release. Plain text works. Keep the formatting simple, because the wire strips most of it anyway.
  5. Add the two images. A real photograph of the practice, the team or the person in the story. Not a stock photo of a model with perfect teeth, which is what the wire is full of and what a reporter will ignore.
  6. Add the video link if you have one. YouTube is accepted and it is a reasonable place to put a thirty second practice tour.
  7. Set the dateline location to your actual city and state. This is a genuine local signal and people get it wrong by defaulting to the nearest big city.
  8. Submit and pay. Editorial review follows.

The link decision, in detail

You get to include links. How you use them matters more than how many you use.

  1. Two links is plenty. One to your homepage, one to the single most relevant service page.
  2. Use plain anchor text. Your practice name, or a natural phrase like "the practice website". Not "best dental implants in [city]".
  3. The reason is directly in Google's spam policy, which names "links with optimized anchor text" in press releases specifically. Optimised anchors in a paid release are the pattern the policy describes.
  4. Do not link to a page that is not ready. A release sends a small amount of real traffic and a larger amount of scrutiny. Both land on whatever page you pointed at.
Save the report

The placement report is the artefact, and most practices never think about it again after the first look.

It is what you show a prospective implant patient who asks about the practice. It is what goes in the practice newsletter. It is what the "as featured in" strip on your homepage is built from. The distribution is a one off. The report is permanent.

Using the placements, which is where the value actually is

The most common failure is not a bad release. It is a perfectly good release that gets distributed and then forgotten about.

The fourth row on that table is worth more than the other five combined, and it takes fifteen minutes.

Building the "as featured in" strip properly

This is the single most durable asset that comes out of a distribution, and most practices build it badly or not at all.

  1. Pick five to eight placements from the report. Choose by recognisability, not by authority score. A local viewer knows what Fox and NBC look like. They have no idea what a domain authority of 87 means.
  2. Use the outlet name or logo, and link each one to the actual published article, not to the outlet homepage. A strip that links nowhere reads as decoration. A strip that links to real articles reads as proof.
  3. Place it below the fold on the homepage and, more importantly, on your highest value service pages. Somebody researching implants needs the reassurance more than somebody looking for a cleaning.
  4. Label it accurately. "Featured in" or "In the news" is fine. "As recommended by" is not, because they did not recommend you, they published your release.
  5. Check the links once a year. Placements do disappear, and a broken credibility strip is worse than no strip.
One line you should not cross

A paid syndicated placement is not editorial endorsement, and presenting it as one is a misrepresentation that a patient could reasonably object to.

"Featured in" describes what happened. "Voted best dentist by Yahoo Finance" does not, and for a healthcare provider that kind of claim carries risk beyond the marketing question.

Set a baseline the week before you distribute. Branded impressions, direct sessions, and the quoted name result count. Without that you have nothing to compare against, and the temptation to attribute every good thing that happened that month to the release becomes irresistible.

The local reporter email, in detail

The wire gets your story onto aggregator sites. It does not put it in front of a human who might write about it. That requires a person emailing a person.

  1. Find two or three reporters who cover business, health or community news for your local paper, local news site or regional magazine. Their bylines are on the site.
  2. Email each one individually. Not a group email, not a blast.
  3. Two sentences on why this matters to their readers specifically, then the release pasted below. Not attached. Pasted, so they can read it without opening anything.
  4. Offer the thing a wire cannot: an interview, a photograph, a walk through of the practice, access to the data behind the story.
  5. Do it on the day the release goes live, while it is still new.

Cision surveyed over three thousand journalists across nineteen markets and found that seventy-two percent named press releases as the most useful thing a PR contact can provide. They also found that eighty-six percent will immediately reject a pitch that is not aligned with their beat or audience.

SourceCision, 2025 State of the Media Report, 3,000+ journalists across 19 markets · www.cision.com/about/press-releases/2025-press-releases/cision…

Both halves of that matter. Reporters want releases. They reject ones that are not relevant to them. Which means the personalised email to the right person beats the untargeted blast every time, and it is the only part of this process that can produce a genuine editorial link.

Putting it in context

The last question is whether $399 on a press release beats $399 spent another way. Here is the comparison honestly.

These are not substitutes. They do different jobs on different timescales.

The ads produce patients this week and nothing the month after you stop. The blog post produces nothing this quarter and then compounds for years if it ranks. The release produces no direct patients at all, and produces credibility, entity signal and an asset you can show people.

A practice doing only one of those three is leaving something on the table. A practice doing the press release first, before the website and the profile are in order, has the order wrong.

An annual rhythm that works

Four releases a year at $399 is $1,596. For most fee for service practices that is a sensible annual line item, and four genuinely newsworthy things do happen in a practice every year if somebody is looking for them.

The discipline is in the "if somebody is looking". Awards get won and nobody writes them up. New equipment arrives and nobody mentions it. An associate joins and it appears only on the website team page. Each of those was a release and each one passed unused.

Before you spend the money

The order of operations is the thing to get right.

A press release amplifies whatever someone finds when they look you up. If what they find is a slow website, a half finished Google Business Profile and eleven reviews from 2021, you have paid $399 to send more people to a worse first impression than they would otherwise have had.
