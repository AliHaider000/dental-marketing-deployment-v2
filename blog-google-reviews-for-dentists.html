Google Reviews for Dentists: Get More Every Month, Reply Without a HIPAA Problem

A weekly system your front desk can run without me: who asks, when, with which link, and how to answer every review, good or bad, without confirming that the reviewer is your patient.

By Max Lezginov13 min readHand this one to the front deskNo gating, no gift cards, no quotasPublished 20 October 2026

In BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, published in February 2026, 74% of US consumers said they look for reviews written in the last three months, and 47% said they will not use a business with fewer than 20 reviews. For a dental practice, that turns reviews from a trophy you win once into a flow you have to keep running every week.

BrightLocal sells local SEO and review software. It surveyed 1,002 US adults through a SurveyMonkey panel, gives no fieldwork dates on the page, and asked about all local businesses, not dentists. For a healthcare read I use Tebra's 6th annual Patient Perspectives Report, an online survey of 3,964 US adults fielded on June 29, 2025. Tebra sells practice management software, so treat both as vendor research.

Getting reviews is half the job. The other half is replying, and that is the half where dental practices have actually been fined. This guide covers both: a request system your front desk can run, a weekly routine, a monthly target with the math shown, reply templates that never confirm anyone is a patient, and what to do when a review is fake.

One disclosure before we start: review generation is part of what I sell. Everything below is the do-it-yourself version, and it works without me.

Quick answer

Ask every patient the same way: a short mention in the chair, then a text with your direct Google review link the same day, and one reminder. No incentives, no filtering, no staff quotas. Aim for at least one new review a week. Reply to every review within a few days, never confirming the reviewer is a patient.

Key takeaways
  • Recency beats volume. 74% of consumers look for reviews written in the last three months (BrightLocal, 2026).
  • Four stars is the floor. 53% of patients will not consider a provider rated under 4 stars (Tebra, 2025).
  • Replies change minds. 70% of respondents in Tebra's 2025 survey said a provider's reply to a negative review improved their opinion.
  • Replies are also where practices get fined. HHS imposed a $50,000 civil money penalty on a North Carolina dental practice that disclosed a patient's information in response to a negative online review (HHS, 2022).
  • The rules are federal now. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews, in force since October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, sentiment-conditioned incentives, undisclosed insider reviews and review suppression (FTC).

Why do Google reviews matter so much for a dental practice?

Reviews do two jobs at once: they help decide whether you appear in the map pack, and they decide whether the patient who sees you there calls. Google says it directly: "More reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking." Reviews sit under what Google calls prominence, alongside how many websites link to you (Google Business Profile Help).

I covered the ranking side, including where rating, count and recency land in the expert surveys, in the Google Business Profile guide for dentists. This guide is about the second job: the shortlist.

Patients compare three or four practices on one screen. Stars and review count are the first filter, before your photos, your website or your fees. Tebra's survey is the closest thing we have to healthcare-specific data, and it lines up with BrightLocal's broader numbers. The wider picture of how patients decide is in how patients choose a dentist in 2026.

SourceTebra, 2025 Patient Perspectives Report (6th annual) · www.tebra.com/theintake/patient-experience/tips-and-trends…

The bar is also rising. BrightLocal asked the same star rating question in 2025 and 2026, and the share of consumers who will only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher nearly doubled in one year.

SourceBrightLocal, Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, n=1,002 US adults · www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/

There is a newer reason to care about what reviews say, not only how many there are. Platforms like Google now use AI to summarize reviews above the individual ones, and BrightLocal found 82% of consumers read those summaries. The summary is built from your patients' own words, which is one more reason never to script them. I cover the wider shift in AI search for dentists.

What this means for your practice

Look up your rating and count the way a patient does: search your practice name on your phone, signed out of Google. If you are under 4.5 stars or under 20 reviews, the fix is not a cleverer reply strategy.

It is asking every patient. Most patients who had an ordinary good visit were simply never asked.

How many new reviews a month should a dental practice aim for?

My rule of thumb is at least one new Google review every week, which works out to about four or five a month. That is not an industry benchmark, because nobody publishes a credible one for dentists. It is what falls out of three consumer expectations in the BrightLocal data.

SourceBrightLocal, Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, n=1,002 US adults · www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/

There is also a reason not to overshoot. Google's Maps content policy treats content showing "unusual volumes or patterns of review contributions" that point to manipulation as rating manipulation. Thirty reviews in one week after two years of silence is exactly that pattern. Steady beats bursty, for patients and for Google.

If you see a lot of patients, you will clear one a week easily, and that is fine as long as the flow stays steady. The rule is a floor, not a ceiling.

Work out your own number

Your monthly target is 4 or 5 reviews, or more if your patient volume allows. The asks you need equal the target divided by your completion rate: the share of asked patients who actually post. Measure that rate in your first month, because nobody can give it to you.

Illustrative example: a practice sends 40 review texts in a month and gets 6 reviews. Its completion rate is 6 ÷ 40 = 15%. To reach 5 reviews a month it needs about 5 ÷ 0.15 = 34 asks, or roughly 8 a week. Almost any practice completes more than 8 visits a week, which is why asking every patient is enough.

What this means for your practice: write "one review a week, minimum" on the front desk board and check it every Friday. Hold it for a quarter and you pass both recency tests; how fast you pass the 20-review test depends on where you start.

Who should ask for reviews, and when?

One named person asks at the end of the visit, and a text with the direct link follows the same day. Everything else is detail. In most practices the ask fails not because patients say no, but because nobody owns it.

Patients are used to being asked. BrightLocal found 78% of consumers were asked to write a review in the last 12 months, and 28% say they will always write one when asked. What they are not used to is a link that takes four taps to find the review box.

Two details matter more than they look. The script says "an honest review", never "a five-star review": asking for a rating or for specific content breaks Google's rules, and the honest version is the one you can defend. And the text goes out the same day, while the visit is fresh and the phone is still in the patient's hand.

A QR code card at the desk is fine as a backup, since Google's own help page suggests printed codes. Just do not stand over anyone while they scan it.

What this means for your practice: the system costs nothing but a named owner and a saved link. If you change one thing this week, give the ask to one person and put it in their checkout routine.

What are the rules on asking for reviews?

Two sets of rules apply, and you follow the stricter one. The FTC's Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465, has been in force since October 21, 2024 (Federal Register). Google's Maps content policy sits on top of it and is stricter on incentives.

The FTC rule, announced on August 14, 2024, bans fake reviews, incentives "conditioned on the writing of consumer reviews expressing a particular sentiment", undisclosed reviews by officers and managers, and review suppression through "unfounded or groundless legal threats, physical threats, intimidation, or certain false public accusations". It also lets the FTC seek civil penalties against knowing violators.

Google goes further. Its policy on incentivized reviews covers reviews "influenced by a payment, discount, free goods or services, or any other benefit", and its main policy bans incentives in exchange for posting any review. Note the word any. The FTC's guide for marketers allows an incentive that is not tied to sentiment, if it is disclosed. Google does not, so a gift card for an honest review is still a violation on Google.

SourceGoogle Maps User Generated Content Policy; eCFR, 16 CFR Part 465; FTC Guide for Marketers · support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en
The two violations friendly practices commit

The first is the staff quota. A bonus for every review a hygienist brings in feels like motivation, but Google lists "merchants requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews" as pressure it does not allow.

The second is "mention Jenny in your review". Google also lists asking for reviews with specific content, including content that identifies a staff member. Thank Jenny in person instead.

What this means for your practice: read your current review process against that table today. If anything in it says "happy patients", "discount" or "quota", rewrite it before the next review comes in.

What does a weekly review routine look like?

About 30 minutes a week, split between the front desk and one manager. The asking happens every day; the reminders, replies and counting happen on fixed days, so they survive a busy week.

Two reply days a week is not arbitrary. BrightLocal found 81% of consumers expect a reply within a week, and 32% want one by the following day. Twice a week keeps every reply inside a few days without anyone living in the review inbox.

The routine is deliberately boring. Review flow stops for one reason more than any other in the practices I work with: the person who did it left, and nobody wrote it down. Print the timeline and tape it inside the front desk drawer.

What this means for your practice: put a name, and a backup name, next to each row of the timeline before Monday.

How do you reply to a review without breaking HIPAA?

Reply to every review, but never confirm that the reviewer is your patient, and never add a detail they did not post. That rules out their treatment, their appointment, their insurance and their bill. A reply that says "we're sorry your crown didn't fit" has just told the internet who your patient is and what you did.

Not replying is not the safe option either. BrightLocal found 89% of consumers expect businesses to respond to reviews, and 42% say they are unlikely to use a business that never replies. Google notifies the reviewer when you reply, so your words are the first thing they hear from you after a bad day (Google Business Profile Help).

The reason for the caution is in the definition. HHS's summary of the HIPAA Privacy Rule describes individually identifiable health information as information that relates to "the provision of health care to the individual" and identifies them. A public reply that confirms a named reviewer received care from you can fall inside that definition, even though the reviewer posted first. Your compliance adviser should confirm how it applies to you; I write marketing, not legal advice.

This is not theoretical. HHS's Office for Civil Rights has taken action against at least three dental practices over replies to online reviews.

SourceHHS Office for Civil Rights, HIPAA resolution agreements and civil money penalties · www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement…

Announcing the New Vision Dental settlement on December 14, 2022, OCR Director Melanie Fontes Rainer put it plainly: "Providers cannot disclose protected health information of their patients when responding to negative online reviews. This is a clear NO."

The fines are small next to what comes with them: corrective action plans, policy rewrites and a page on hhs.gov with the practice's name on it, still online years later. In the Elite Dental Associates agreement, OCR also found the practice had not implemented policies and procedures, including for releasing patient information on social media.

The "never our patient" row catches people out. If you deny it for strangers and stay silent for real patients, the difference itself gives the answer away. Use one neutral line for everyone.

What should you do about fake or unfair reviews?

Flag it through Google if it breaks Google's rules, reply once and calmly if it does not, and never retaliate. Google's position is blunt: "Google doesn't get involved in conflict between businesses and customers", and only reviews that violate its policies are eligible for removal (Google Business Profile Help).

So "this is unfair" is not a reason Google will act on. A review from someone who was never at the practice, a competitor, a current or former employee, an account posting personal information, or plain abuse can be. Google's conflict of interest rule names former employment and industry competitors specifically.

  1. In your Business Profile, open Read reviews, choose Report next to the review, pick the reason and send it.
  2. Track it in Google's Reviews Management Tool. Google says evaluation typically takes several days.
  3. If the result is "no policy violation", you get a one-time appeal through the same tool.
  4. While you wait, reply once with the neutral template. Do not mention that you reported it.
  5. Keep a screenshot and the date in your review log, in case the pattern repeats.

What not to do is just as specific. The FTC rule bans unfounded legal threats, intimidation and false public accusations aimed at getting a review removed, so a lawyer's letter over an honest one-star review is the wrong move. Replying with the patient's history to prove them wrong is the HHS problem from the previous section.

Do not answer a wave of fake negatives with a wave of friendly positives either. A sudden burst is the pattern Google's rating manipulation policy describes, so the cure becomes a second problem.

One honest negative is not a disaster

Google's own tips page makes a point owners rarely believe: "A mix of positive and negative feedback often feels more trustworthy."

A 4.8 with a few calm, well-answered complaints reads as real. A wall of perfect fives with no replies reads as managed.

What this means for your practice: decide now who may flag reviews and who may answer negative ones. In most practices that should be one manager, not whoever is most annoyed that day.

Which review numbers should you track each month?

Five numbers, checked monthly, tell you whether the system is running: new reviews, rating, recency, reply rate and review link clicks. Add asks sent from the front desk log, and you have everything you need.

SourceBrightLocal, Local Consumer Review Survey 2026 (thresholds) · www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/

Review link clicks are the number most practices cannot see, and the one that separates "patients are not getting the text" from "patients get it and do not finish". If clicks rise and reviews stay flat, look at the review screen itself: Google requires reviewers to be signed in to a Google account, and some patients stop there.

Count clicks with a link you control, either a link shortener that reports clicks or a tracked button on a page of your own site. The tag setup is the same as in my tracking guide for dental practices.

Frequently asked questions

Can we use review software that sends a satisfaction survey first?
Only if every patient gets the Google link whatever they answer. If the software sends happy patients to Google and unhappy ones to a private form, that is selective solicitation, which Google's policy does not allow, and the FTC's guide for marketers warns against asking only customers you expect to be positive. Check the routing settings in any tool before you switch it on.
Can we reply to a review that names the dentist and the procedure?
Yes, but do not repeat it or add to it. Reviewers can share their own information; your reply should not confirm it. Thank them for the feedback, say you cannot discuss anyone's care publicly, and give a name and a number. Sign it from the office manager rather than the treating dentist, so the reply does not read as a clinical response.
Should we ask for reviews on Yelp and Facebook too?
Pick one primary platform and put the effort there. For most US practices that is Google, because it feeds the map pack, and BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 45% of consumers left a Google review in the past year, more than on any other site. In my experience one link gets more finished reviews than a menu of three.
Is it fine to text patients the review link?
Yes, through the same system you use for appointment reminders, to patients who have agreed to receive texts from you. Keep treatment details out of the message, since phones get read by other people, and send one reminder at most. Google's help page lists email and messaging apps among the ways to share the link.
What if a former employee or a competitor leaves a review?
Report it. Google's policy treats content based on a conflict of interest, including current or former employment and industry competitors, as rating manipulation, which is not allowed on Maps. Use Report on the review, then track it in the Reviews Management Tool and appeal once if needed. Reply only if it stays up, with the neutral template, and never name the person.
Do review replies help rankings?
Google's ranking help page says more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking, and that replying shows you value feedback. It does not say replies raise rankings, so do not stuff keywords or city names into them. Write each reply for the next patient reading it, who in Tebra's survey is likely to think better of a provider that answers criticism well.
